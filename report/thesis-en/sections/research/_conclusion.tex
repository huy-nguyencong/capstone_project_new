Attribute-level evidence, rendered as text and reasoned over by a small LLM,
recovers 28 of the 67 identities that CLIP alone ranks outside its top 10, a gain
of 23 points in R@10, with no fine-tuning and on a pool produced by CLIP itself.
The gain does not come from a better similarity measure but from making the
evidence readable: what this crop read, what the rest of the track read, how
strongly those frames link to each other, and under what imaging conditions each
reading was made. Measured directly, the similarity score that the system does not
use for ranking separates the ground truth from the rest of its own pool with an
AUC of 0.601: CLIP retrieves well but ranks the retrieved pool poorly.

The cost is small: five of the 33 identities already found by retrieval are pushed
out, and four of those five are recovered when only the two ranking calls are
given a larger model over the same evidence. Most of what remains is not a ranking
problem. Three of 100 identities were never retrieved, and the rest of the
remainder comes from attributes that the describer could not read or did not
write. Both causes are measurable, and this part reports the ceiling next to every
number, which gives a clearer starting point for further work than the one the
project began with.
