\chapter{Attribute Index and Multi-Agent Reasoning}
\label{chapter:r-reasoning}

\textit{This chapter describes the attribute index built offline with a vision-language model, and the multi-agent funnel that re-ranks the candidates of CLIP retrieval from that evidence.}

\section{The Attribute Index}

\subsection{Schema}
Each described crop carries the 18 attributes of Table~\ref{tab:r-attrs},
centred on garments because that is what witnesses describe.
\at{carrying\_canon} is list-valued (a backpack \emph{and} a handbag); the others
are single-valued per crop, but the same attribute read across a track often
yields several values, which is the signal the reasoning stages use.

\midrule

\subsection{Visual context}
Attributes alone cannot explain a disagreement: if one frame reads the top as red
and another as black, the question is whether the capture conditions make this
confusion likely. Every crop therefore also carries quality statistics per band:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Band} & \textbf{Extent of the crop}\\
\midrule
\at{whole} & the entire crop\\
\at{head}  & the first 15\%\\
\at{upper} & the next 30\%\\
\at{lower} & the next 40\%\\
\at{feet}  & the last 15\%\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Each band has four numbers: sharpness, contrast, brightness and saturation. Since
\at{upper\_inner\_color} is routed to the \at{upper} band, a note on a colour
disagreement can cite the upper-body saturation of the frames that read red and of
those that read black: a mechanism with its figure rather than a guess.

\subsection{Three different counts}
A value can be counted in three ways, the easiest confusion in this design, so the
implementation keeps three separate functions:

\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{global prior} is the value's share over the \emph{whole index},
      with lists \emph{split}: readings \at{[red, white]} and \at{red} give red
      66\% and white 33\% over three observations. It is the base rate for rarity.
\item The \emph{track distribution} keeps one track's readings \emph{whole}: the
      same readings give ``red+white 50\%, red 50\%''. The evidence reader quotes it.
\item \emph{Membership} tells whether a query value \at{red} matches a frame that
      read \at{[red, white]}, which it does.
\end{itemize}

The local (pool) prior is computed over \emph{deduplicated tracks}, not pool crops:
two crops of one five-frame track give a five-frame statistic, not a ten-frame
one. Otherwise a person retrieved many times would dominate the pool statistic.

\subsection{The image-quality problem, and why voting does not work}
\label{sec:r-noise}
Table~\ref{tab:r-noise} shows one real track described over ten frames, the
motivating example for Section~\ref{sec:r-reasoning}.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Attribute} & \textbf{Across the 10 described frames}\\
\midrule
\at{gender}             & female 80\%, male 20\%\\
\at{age\_group}         & young adult 100\%\\
\at{build}              & slim 100\%\\
\at{height\_class}      & average height 80\%, average 20\%\\
\at{hair\_color}        & black 40\%, brown 30\%, blonde 30\% (split)\\
\at{hair\_style}        & short 50\%, straight 30\%, long+straight 20\% (split)\\
\at{upper\_outer\_type} & hoodie 20\%, not visible 80\% (split)\\
\at{upper\_outer\_color}& black 20\%, not visible 80\% (split)\\
\at{upper\_inner\_type} & sweater 70\%, tank top 10\%, not visible 20\%\\
\at{upper\_inner\_color}& gray 40\%, black 40\%, not visible 20\%
                          (tied, the aggregate is arbitrary)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption[VLM noise on one track]{VLM noise on one track. A majority vote would report
\at{hair\_color}${=}$black on a 40/30/30 split, and would break the 40/40 tie
on \at{upper\_inner\_color} by whichever value the sort visited first.}
\label{tab:r-noise}
\end{table}

The track shows three distinct problems. Ties make the aggregate arbitrary. Splits
across near-synonyms (\at{short}, \at{straight}, \at{long+straight}) disagree about
vocabulary, not about the person. Occlusion (\at{not visible} 80\%) is the absence
of an observation, not a value, and counting it would let an invisible garment
outvote a visible one. A vote cannot tell these cases apart; an LLM told what each
form means can.

\section{Multi-Agent Semantic Reasoning}
\label{sec:r-reasoning}

\subsection{The funnel}
The reasoning block (Figure~\ref{fig:r-reasoning}) keeps ZARA's coarse-to-fine
iteration (Section~\ref{sec:r-zara}), pruning before deciding, with four agents
over two rounds:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The attribute-value selector (stage 1) chooses the pairs to spend evidence
      on, from the knowledge base and the pool statistics.
\item The evidence pruning agent (stage 2A) reads, for every candidate and pair,
      what this crop says and the evidence around that reading.
\item After the coarse cut ($75 \rightarrow 25$), a second selector (stage 3)
      works on the survivors, whose statistics have changed with the pool.
\item The decision agent (stages 2B and 4B) ranks the candidates from the notes
      and returns the final ordering.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{S36-reasoning-block.png}
\caption[The semantic reasoning block]{The semantic reasoning block. The knowledge base supplies per-attribute
statistics to both selector calls; the text query reaches the pruning and
decision agents directly.}
\label{fig:r-reasoning}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why stage 0 is not in the funnel.} The query analyst (stage 0) is
deliberately left out of the list and the figure: the funnel looks at candidates,
and stage 0 never sees one. It is a \emph{pre-run} over the query alone, once per
query, settling how each query word relates to each index value. Its output is a
lookup table read by every stage of both rounds, not a decision about any
candidate, so drawing it as a fifth agent would wrongly suggest it prunes or ranks
(Section~\ref{sec:r-stage0}).

The prompts themselves are not reproduced; the rules that shape each stage are
described below. Two properties drive their design. First, the coarse cut cannot
be undone: a candidate placed 26th in round one is lost, while one ranked low in
round two can still be returned, and the ranker is told this asymmetry in
round-dependent terms (Section~\ref{sec:r-coverage}). Second, round two renumbers
the survivors as candidates $1..M$ without their round-one labels, so the refine
round is not anchored by the coarse ordering.

\subsection{Stage 0: query analyst}
\label{sec:r-stage0}
Before any candidate is seen, one call settles what the query asks of each
attribute: for every attribute in the index vocabulary it emits either
\code{NOT-CONSTRAINED} or one line per (index value, query value) pair with a
relation:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Relation} & \textbf{Meaning}\\
\midrule
\code{exact}     & interchangeable both ways; nothing gained or lost by writing either\\
\code{near}      & a plausible neighbour: what a describer might write for the same
                   thing\\
                 & under different light, from further away, or in motion\\
\code{far}       & a different value of the same kind, unlikely to be confused\\
\code{unrelated} & not the same property\\
\code{neutral}   & can neither confirm nor contradict (hides the attribute, too vague)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
This is a statement about \emph{words}: the analyst sees no candidate, image or
statistic.

\paragraph{Why a separate pre-run.} Every later stage must judge whether a crop's
reading agrees with the query: the selector to weigh a pair, the evidence reader to
place the query value, the ranker to size a conflict. Without stage 0, each call
would derive the relation between, say, \emph{navy} and \emph{black} on its own,
inside a prompt crowded with statistics, and the reader could treat a reading as a
near match while the ranker treats it as a contradiction. Settling the relations
once, from words alone, gives:

\begin{itemize}
\item Consistency: one relation per pair, read the same way by stages 1 to 4 in
      both rounds, instead of possibly different answers to the same question.
\item Separation of concerns: deciding a relation without candidates, images or
      statistics keeps it free of bias from value frequency or pool content, so
      later stages reason about evidence, not vocabulary.
\item Cost: the call depends only on the query and the vocabulary, so it is small,
      runs once and is replayed from disk on reruns (Section~\ref{sec:r-engineering}).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The most useful relation is \code{near}: it lets the reader and the ranker treat a
likely describer confusion (navy read as black in a dim crop) differently from a
real mismatch (navy read as red).

\subsection{Stage 1: attribute-value selector}
The selector chooses 4 to 10 attribute=value pairs with the highest expected
decision value, each with a one-sentence reason (Figure~\ref{fig:r-selector}). It
sees the \emph{global prior}, the value's share over the whole index, and the
\emph{local prior}, the pool's distribution with mean CLIP similarity and track
stability per value (Figure~\ref{fig:r-selector-work}).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{S37-selector.png}
\caption[The attribute-value selector]{The selector in the abstract. It decides \emph{where to look}, never what
is true: the output is a set of pairs with reasons, not a verdict.}
\label{fig:r-selector}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{S38-selector-example.png}
\caption[The selector with its real inputs and output]{The same stage with its real inputs and output: the two prior tables as
input, an attribute-value pair with its reason as output.}
\label{fig:r-selector-work}
\end{figure}

The choice must weigh five dimensions jointly, none deciding alone: the stage-0
relation; discrimination, how small a subset the value picks out; partitioning, how
well it separates competing groups; inspectability, track stability qualified by
track size, since a single-crop track has stability 1.00 by construction; and
enrichment or depletion. A rare exact value can be decisive, a common one weak, and
a \emph{near} value worth a slot because it explains an apparent mismatch.

After measurement, the selector's input was restricted to the attributes the query
constrains: shown the whole index, it spent slots on unmentioned attributes, for
which any reading fits the request equally.

\subsection{Stage 2A: evidence reader}
This is the most expensive stage and the core of the system. For each candidate
and pair it writes exactly two lines: what \emph{this crop} read, and a note on the
evidence around that reading. Figure~\ref{fig:r-pruning} shows it with the
following ranker (stage 2B), and Figure~\ref{fig:r-evidence} both on a real example.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{S39-evidence-ranker.png}
\caption[The evidence and decision stages]{The evidence and decision stages in the abstract: stage A reads the
evidence per candidate and per attribute, and stage B ranks from what it wrote.}
\label{fig:r-pruning}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{S40-evidence-example.png}
\caption[The evidence and decision stages at work]{The same two stages at work: one rendered evidence block, the note the
reader writes from it, and the ranking the decision agent produces. Every figure
in the note appears in the block; nothing is recomputed or invented.}
\label{fig:r-evidence}
\end{figure}

The rendered block for one candidate and pair contains at most:

\begin{itemize}
\item the crop's reading, with its relation to the query value and its index share,
      for example \code{short [near] [index 65\%]};
\item the track distribution, kept whole, such as \code{short 8/10 = 80\% repeated}
      and \code{long 2/10 = 20\% repeated};
\item linkage and cohesion: how strongly the frames reading this value link to the
      crop (\code{vs crop 0.57 +/- 0.07}) and to each other
      (\code{together 0.73 +/- 0.14}), with the number of pairs behind each figure;
\item conditions: the quality figures of the relevant band with their deviation
      from the pool, for example \code{sharp 21.5 (-1.5) mid},
      \code{contrast 120.7 (+14.7) mid}, \code{bright 88.3 (+16.2) mid},
      \code{sat 110.9 (-48.1) mid};
\item the view: whether this frame is unusual for its value, printed only when at
      least two metrics fall outside that value's range;
\item frame agreement: how much a quoted frame resembles the crop \emph{excluding
      the attribute being judged}, so agreement on the upper colour cannot justify
      trusting it about the upper colour.
\end{itemize}

Four rules govern the note, each adopted after a measured failure:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The citation rule: never name a mechanism without the figure that shows it.
      This cut invented causes from 19 to 0 and mechanisms without numbers to 0 of
      82, the clearest result in the pipeline; it was also applied to the ranker.
\item The crop is a reference observation, not the truth: a frame agreeing with it
      proves nothing, since a tracker can group two people.
\item No deciding, ranking or averaging: the reader may not write \emph{correct},
      \emph{winner}, \emph{most likely} or \emph{therefore the person is X}; it
      makes the evidence readable, and another stage orders it.
\item A length budget: at most 25 words for an uncontested unit and 40 to 95 words
      for a contested one, written in full with two to four channels and their
      figures, then a sentence on where the evidence leaves the readings.
\end{enumerate}

A single call with 75 candidates would exceed the free tier's token window, so
stage 2A is split into three paced calls of 25 (Section~\ref{sec:r-engineering}),
which is sound because no note depends on another.

\subsection{Stage 2B: the ranker}
The ranker sees only the query and one note per attribute per candidate: no
per-attribute verdict, score or CLIP similarity. It ranks all $N$ candidates and
writes at least $N_{\text{keep}} = \text{cap} + 5$ rows (30 of 75 in the coarse
round, 15 of 25 in the refine round) as a markdown table with one reason each;
$N_{\text{keep}}$ is a minimum, and the model keeps writing while the evidence
still separates candidates. Three mechanisms shape the ordering.

\paragraph{Reliability, not direction.} Whether an attribute matches comes first,
but the ordering rests on how much weight each reading can bear. Ranking on
direction made 69 of 75 rows open with ``Exact red top, shorts, female gender'',
shared by the whole pool and ordering nothing.

\paragraph{What each query value is worth.} A rarity table at the top of the
message gives, for each query value, its index share, the \emph{typical} (median)
share of its attribute, the most common share and the track stability, with a
label, \emph{decisive} ($\geq 5\times$ typical), \emph{moderate}
($\geq 1.5\times$) or \emph{almost nothing}, and the ratio printed for checking. It
was added after a measured failure: a candidate whose upper body was read as green
in every frame, a rare colour, was ranked below candidates that only matched a
common colour, because no stage carried any notion of rarity.

\paragraph{The coverage floor.}
\label{sec:r-coverage}
The two rounds get different instructions about absence, because their cuts
differ in kind.

\begin{itemize}
\item In the coarse round, PRESENT means read \emph{anywhere}: in one frame, as a
      minority or overshadowed reading, in the track but not this crop, or as a
      \code{[near]} value. A present but weak reading ranks above an absent one.
      ABSENT is either \emph{read nowhere in the track} (silence) or \emph{nothing
      closer than \code{[far]} read anywhere} (contradiction). The floor applies
      only to the absence of a value the rarity table calls \emph{decisive}; it is
      neither a count of matches nor a score.
\item In the final round nothing lies below the cut to protect. A value read
      nowhere still pushes a candidate down; a single overshadowed frame is weak
      positive evidence, weighed with the rest, not a floor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What the CLIP score is worth inside the pool}
\label{sec:r-clipscore}
The ranker deliberately does \emph{not} receive the CLIP similarity, a decision
based on Table~\ref{tab:r-clipscore}. CLIP does two jobs here. Building the pool
is not in question: the 75 candidates are its top 75 of the whole index. Ordering
inside the pool is: the pool is already the narrow top tail of the similarity
distribution, so the spread that ranked it against the \emph{index} may be almost
gone \emph{within} it.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Measured over the evaluated pools} & \textbf{Value}\\
\midrule
Mean pool similarity                        & 0.3272\\
Mean within-pool spread (rank 1 $-$ rank 75)& 0.0622\\
Mean within-pool standard deviation         & 0.0142\\
Median adjacent-rank gap                    & 0.0004 \ (2.7\% of one sd)\\
AUC, ground truth vs.\ rest of pool         & 0.601\\
Ground-truth $z$-score within pool          & $+1.24$ sd\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption[What the CLIP score separates inside the pool]{What the CLIP score separates \emph{inside} the 75-candidate pool. The AUC
is the probability that a ground-truth crop outranks a non-ground-truth crop there;
1.0 would mean the score alone sorts the pool perfectly, and 0.5 would be chance.
It is computed with ties taken into account, because many crops share the same
similarity to three decimals and counting a tie as a win would overstate the score
exactly where it is weakest.}
\label{tab:r-clipscore}
\end{table}

All three measurements agree. An AUC of 0.601 is closer to chance than to a useful
ordering; the median gap between \emph{adjacent} ranks is 2.7\% of one standard
deviation, so ranks 8 and 20 differ by noise; and the ground truth sits $+1.24$
standard deviations above the pool mean, above average but not separated. CLIP is
thus a good \emph{retriever}, having found this pool among 496 identities, but a
weak \emph{ranker of what it retrieved}. Passing its score to the ranker would add
little information and anchor it to the ordering it is meant to correct.

\subsection{Stages 3 and 4}
Stage 3 is the stage-1 prompt plus one paragraph, selecting pairs again over the
25 survivors, whose statistics differ: a value rare in the full pool may now be
shared by all survivors and useless for separating them. Stage 4A reruns the
evidence reader on the new pairs, and stage 4B is the ranker with the
\code{COVERAGE\_FINAL} section; its output, cut to 10, is the answer.

\subsection{Engineering}
\label{sec:r-engineering}
Three implementation details matter for reproducibility:

\begin{itemize}
\item Replay by byte-identical input. Every stage saves its rendered input and
      reply; a rerun replays each stage whose input is byte-identical and resumes
      at the first stage without a saved answer, so a run stopped by a quota limit
      continues without repeating calls.
\item Pacing. A rolling token-per-minute pacer holds back calls that would exceed
      the window, with 60 seconds between 2A batches, 180 seconds between
      identities, and exponential retry (60/120/240/480\,s) on errors 503 and 429.
\item Robust parsing. Duplicate rows and short replies are reported as different
      failures; a candidate skipped mid-list is appended in input order rather than
      dropped; and an unparsable ranking falls back to the CLIP order with a
      warning, since an empty round would score like a wrong answer while hiding
      the failure.
\end{itemize}
